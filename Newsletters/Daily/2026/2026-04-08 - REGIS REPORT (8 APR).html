REGIS REPORT

08 April 2026

With CPI inflation hitting 4.1% for March even just in the early period of the energy crisis it’s now looking likely that it will exceed 5% by April. The full effect of the huge price jumps at the pump have yet to feed through to other items in the rest of the basket. Once it does, CPI inflation should be well above 5%.

 

The Bangko Sentral (BSP) came out with a statement immediately after the data release, saying it will “carefully consider” incoming data at its next Monetary Board meeting two weeks from now. The BSP is caught between a rock and a hard place right now. Inflation is climbing sharply due to global events out of its control. While a rate increase may appear to be the proper textbook response, it probably realizes that such a move will be futile and may, in fact, exacerbate public misery.

MACRO
Inflation accelerates to 4.1% in March
  • Headline inflation picked up to 4.1% in Mar from Feb's 2.4%
    • This was higher than consensus expectations of 3.8% and above the BSP's forecast range of 3.1-3.9% for Mar
    • The 4.1% print is the highest since Jul 2024 (4.4%) and marks the fourth consecutive month of acceleration in inflation
      • The BSP released a statement on the higher-than-expected inflation print, citing that the central bank will "carefully consider" incoming data at its upcoming meeting given mounting risks to the inflation outlook
        • The second out of six Monetary Board meetings for the year will be held on Apr 23
          • For 2026, consensus and Regis expect rates to be held steady at 4.25%
  • This brings 3M26 inflation to 2.8%, within the BSP's long-term target range of 2-4% and below their forecast of 5.1% for the year
  • Inflation in Mar accelerated given faster YoY increases vs Feb in food, utilities (electricity, gas, fuel), and transport prices
    • Food prices overall rose 3.0% YoY, accelerating from the 1.8% pace in Feb, driven by higher prices of rice and vegetables
      • Rice prices are up 3.6% YoY in Mar, the first YoY price increase after fourteen consecutive months of YoY price declines
      • Rice prices have notably been on the rise to P50/kg and P48/kg as of Apr-26 for local and imported well-milled rice from the low of around P42/kg each back in Oct-25
  • Core inflation inched up slightly to 3.2% in Mar from 2.9% in Feb, driven by core non-food inflation which likewise rose by 3.2% in Mar from 2.9% in Feb
  • For 2026, the BSP forecasts inflation to settle at 5.1% (previously revised from 3.6%)
    • Consensus and Regis expect inflation to be 3.0% and 4.0%

 

 

 

 

 

MACRO
Government posts P171bn budget deficit in Feb
  • The Philippine government recorded a budget deficit of P171bn in Feb, unchanged YoY but a reversal from the P165bn surplus in Jan due to a boost from non-tax revenues and a rebound in spending
    • This brings the 2M26 deficit to P6bn, 94% narrower vs the same period last year
  • Revenue collections in Feb rose 43% YoY, accelerating from Jan's flattish growth (+0.4%), primarily led by non-tax revenues which surged 540% YoY
    • Bureau of Internal Revenue (BIR) collections picked up to +8% from +1% in Jan
    • Customs collections rose a modest 3%, slightly up from 2% growth the previous month
    • Non-tax revenues surged 540% following the early remittance of dividends from GOCCs
      • Dividends from GOCCs were remitted in May last year
  • Expenditures rebounded to growth, up 26% YoY in Feb from the 24% decline in Jan as primary spending reverted back to growth
    • Primary spending for the month surged by 29% YoY
      • This marks first month of YoY growth after six consecutive months of YoY declines in gov't spending
    • Interest payments were broadly flat YoY (+1%)

 

 

 

BANKS
Consumer loans +19.1% in 4Q25, NPLs steady; Credit card loans now bigger than mortgages

Consumer loans grew 19.1% YoY in 4Q25, slowing from the 21.3% pace in 3Q25, but faster than the 18.0% in 2024. The deceleration was seen across all major categories, with only motorcycle loans picking up. Consumer loans ended 2025 at 22.4% of total loans, slightly lower than the 3Q25 figure due to a typical/seasonal surge in corporate loans in 4Q. However, that is still higher than the 21.1% posted at end-2024.  We estimate that consumer loans as a share of GDP blipped up to 13.1%, from 11.7% in 2024. Overall consumer loan NPLs eased slightly to 5.32% from 5.38% in 3Q and 5.34% from end-2004. However, that NPL experience was mixed, with mortgages improving, motor vehicles deteriorating, and credit cards flat.

 

The 2025 data is also significant (in our view) as it is the first time on record that credit card receivables (P1,194.1bn) exceeded mortgage loans (P1,193.7bn).

 

By segment:

  • Mortgage growth slowed sharply to 8.6% YoY in 4Q25, from 10.9% the previous quarter, and from 9.6% in 2024. To the upside, mortgage NPLs improved to 6.08%, down 30bps QoQ and 23bps YoY.
  • Motor Vehicle loans grew 14.4% YoY much slower than the 17.3% in 3Q and the 18.5% pace in 2024. NPL ratio also worsened to 5.05%, up 15bps from the 4.90% in 3Q25, and up 9bps from the 4.96% at end-2024.
    • Auto loan growth slowed sharply to 13.9% YoY, from 17.2% in 3Q, and from 19.6% in 4Q24, while NPLs were up 12bps QoQ to 4.37%, and up 5bps YoY.
    • Motorcycle loans accelerated to +22.9% YoY, compared to 20.6% in 3Q, and much faster than the 1.6% in 2024. NPL ratio was flat at16.16% up 2bps QoQ, but down 15bps from 16.31% in 4QQ24.
  • Credit card receivables expanded 27.8% YoY, decelerating from the 29.5% clip in 3Q25 and 29.4% in 4Q24. Meanwhile, NPL ratio edged down 1bp QoQ to 4.81%, but was up significantly +44bps YoY from 4.37% at end-2024
  • Salary loans surged 36.9% YoY up from the 37.3% YoY in 3Q and the 17.5% YoY the prior year. NPL ratio stood at 4.74%, up 17bps QoQ, but down 63bps YoY from 5.34% in 2024.

 

The relative resilience of consumer loan NPLs is remarkable, given the weak GDP print in 3Q/4Q (4% and 3% respectively). However, we note that in neither quarter were consumers particularly badly hit. The major drive for the GDP slowdown was government spending and government construction. We are wary over the impact of the ongoing Iran conflict on consumers’ wallets, particularly as high fuel prices lead into higher electricity prices, and other consumer goods.
