01 October 2020

Last week, the Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas (BSP) formally imposed caps on interest rates and fees on credit cards. The most significant of these is a maximum finance charge of 24% (ie 2% a month) on unpaid balances (revolvers), almost half of the current rate of 42% (3.5% a month). When we wrote about this potential issue last month, we noted the difficulty in estimating the impact. Our (very) rough estimate now is that the combination of caps could cut pre-tax profit by 6-10% (using 2019 as base). However, this assumes that banks will not adjust business models – raise other fees, reduce rewards/promos for non-revolvers, etc.

The bigger issue – in our view – is that the rate/fee caps set a major precedent. Up until now, the BSP has never, ever, ever regulated pricing of products and services of banks and non-banks. In fact, Section 305 of the Manual on Regulation of Banks (MORB) explicitly says that rates/fees “shall not be subject to any regulatory ceiling.” The recent Circular 1098 adds the exception for credit cards. We believe this opens up further rate/fee regulation by the BSP (and/or the government). Together with loan grace periods, it would seem that the banks are being pressed into national service.

The unintended consequence could be that rate regulation will reverse financial inclusion initiatives, and drive borrowers to the un-regulated money lenders.

The Bangko Sentral (BSP) is set to approve another P540bn advance (interest-free) to the national government after the previous P300b advance was recently repaid. At the current rate, we estimate the BSP would have lent over P1.2trillion to the national government, inclusive of Treasury bill purchases in the secondary market. This would be an increase of P1trn (US$20bn) so far this year. For perspective, total outstanding domestic debt of the government today is P6.7trn.

Moderation does not appear to be in the vocabulary of the current BSP.

We take a look at the continuing count of Covid-19 cases today following the end of Sept (and also 3Q).

Amidst daily new infections of over 2,000 reported, the total number of active cases has actually been declining. With the necessary caveat of saying all of this is based on government data, active cases as of end-Sept stood at under 53,000. This is a modest dip from the end-Aug figure of 59,700, suggesting the spread of Covid-19 is being contained.  The hospital occupancy numbers appear to be showing this as well.

The lower hospital occupancy has emboldened the government to undertake a policy of mandatory quarantine of all infected cases. Which is a nicer way of saying that everyone who tests positive for Covd-19 (asymptomatic, mild or severe cases included) will not be allowed to stay home and will be taken away to a government-designated facility. What do you think will happen to voluntary testing, then?

Watch the daily test numbers over the next few weeks. A drop in tests could signal further divergence between on-the-ground reality and the government statistics that the drive official policy.

The elections are twenty months away but we are already seeing the political maneuverings of a few likely to emerge as key players.

House Speaker Alan Peter Cayetano supposedly offered to resign his speakership yesterday (ostensibly as part of his promise to split his term with Rep Lord Velasco), only for House members to very quickly reject his resignation. Leadership of the House means influence over the budget, which would be crucial to mustering support from local politicians ahead of the 2022 presidential elections which Cayetano is expected to contest.

Meanwhile, the Supreme Court ordered the contending parties in the 2016 Vice Presidential race to submit further comments on Bongbong Marcos’ proposal to expand the recount after the initial recount showed him losing to Vice President Leni Robredo by an even greater margin. It is a wonder why the Court has still not moved to issue a final decision more than four years after the 2016 polls. Both Robredo and Marcos are possible contenders in the 2022 elections.
